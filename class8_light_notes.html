SECTION 01 Introduction to Light
What is Light?
Definition

Light is a form of energy that travels in straight lines and enables us to see objects around us. It is an electromagnetic wave that does not need any medium to travel — it can travel even through vacuum (empty space).

Properties of Light
  • Light travels in a straight line (rectilinear propagation).
  • Speed of light in air/vacuum = 3 × 10⁸ m/s (3,00,000 km per second!)
  • Light can be reflected (bounced back) by smooth surfaces.
  • Light can be refracted (bent) when it passes from one medium to another.
  • Light can be absorbed by dark or opaque objects.
Sources of Light
Luminous Objects

Objects that produce their own light.
Examples: Sun, stars, candle flame, torch, electric bulb, firefly.

Non-Luminous Objects

Objects that do NOT produce their own light. They become visible only when light falls on them.
Examples: Moon, table, book, mirror.

Important Point

The Moon is a non-luminous body. It reflects the sunlight we see. The Sun is a natural luminous source while an electric bulb is an artificial luminous source.

SECTION 02 Reflection of Light & Laws of Reflection
What is Reflection?
Definition

Reflection is the process by which light hits a surface and bounces back into the same medium. Mirrors reflect light and help form images.

Important Terms in Reflection
  • Incident Ray — The ray of light that falls on the mirror surface.
  • Reflected Ray — The ray of light that bounces back after hitting the mirror.
  • Normal — An imaginary line drawn perpendicular (at 90°) to the mirror at the point where the incident ray hits.
  • Angle of Incidence (∠i) — The angle between the incident ray and the normal.
  • Angle of Reflection (∠r) — The angle between the reflected ray and the normal.
  • Point of Incidence — The exact point on the mirror where the incident ray meets the surface.

Laws of Reflection
★ Must Learn — Laws of Reflection

Law 1: The angle of incidence (∠i) is always equal to the angle of reflection (∠r).
∠i = ∠r

Law 2: The incident ray, the reflected ray, and the normal at the point of incidence — all lie in the same plane.

Know More

More than 800 years ago, Indian astronomers in Bhāskara II's time used shallow bowls of water to observe stars. By studying the reflected images in water, they could measure the positions of stars — showing that reflection was understood long before modern science explained it.

Types of Reflection
Regular Reflection

When a beam of parallel light rays falls on a smooth, polished surface (like a mirror), all rays reflect in the same direction — forming a clear, sharp image.
Example: Mirror, still water surface, polished metal.

Diffused / Irregular Reflection

When a beam of parallel light rays falls on a rough, uneven surface, rays reflect in many different directions — no clear image is formed, but the object is still visible.
Example: Paper, wall, cloth, wood.

SECTION 03 Plane Mirror
Definition

A plane mirror is a flat, smooth mirror with a polished reflecting surface. It is the most common type of mirror used in daily life — like the mirror in your bathroom.

Characteristics of Image Formed by a Plane Mirror
  • The image is always virtual (cannot be formed on a screen).
  • The image is always erect / upright (not flipped up or down).
  • The image is of the same size as the object.
  • The image is formed as far behind the mirror as the object is in front of it. (object distance = image distance)
  • The image is laterally inverted — left side appears as right side and right side appears as left.
Lateral Inversion

Lateral inversion means the left and right sides of the image are reversed compared to the object. That is why when you raise your right hand in front of a mirror, your image appears to raise its left hand. Ambulances have their name written mirror-reversed so that drivers see it correctly in their rear-view mirrors.

SECTION 04 Spherical Mirrors

Spherical mirrors are curved mirrors that are a part of a hollow sphere. Unlike plane mirrors which are flat, spherical mirrors have a curved reflecting surface. There are two types of spherical mirrors:

Concave Mirror

A mirror whose reflecting surface curves inward (like the inside of a bowl or the inside of a spoon). Also called a converging mirror because it brings light rays together.

Convex Mirror

A mirror whose reflecting surface curves outward (like the back of a spoon). Also called a diverging mirror because it spreads light rays apart.

Important Terms in Spherical Mirrors
  • Centre of Curvature (C) — The centre of the hollow sphere of which the mirror is a part.
  • Radius of Curvature (R) — The radius of the hollow sphere (distance from pole to centre of curvature).
  • Pole (P) — The midpoint of the reflecting surface of the mirror.
  • Principal Axis — The imaginary line passing through the pole and the centre of curvature, perpendicular to the mirror surface.
  • Focus / Focal Point (F) — The point on the principal axis where parallel rays of light, after reflection, meet (concave) or appear to diverge from (convex). It lies midway between the pole and the centre of curvature.
  • Focal Length (f) — The distance between the pole and the focus. f = R/2

SECTION 05 Characteristics of Images Formed by Spherical Mirrors
Real Image vs Virtual Image
Real Image

Formed when reflected rays actually meet at a point. Can be caught on a screen. Always inverted. Example: image in a concave mirror when object is far away.

Virtual Image

Formed when reflected rays appear to meet behind the mirror (they don't actually meet). Cannot be caught on a screen. Always erect. Example: image in a plane mirror or convex mirror.

Quick Memory Trick:

  • Concave + Close → Large, Upright, Virtual
  • Concave + Far → Small, Inverted, Real
  • Convex + Any → Small, Upright, Virtual (always!)
SECTION 06 Real-Life Uses of Spherical Mirrors
Uses of Concave Mirror
  • Torches and Car Headlights: The concave curved surface reflects light into a strong, focused beam so that light travels far ahead.
  • Dentist's Mirror: Dentists use small concave mirrors to get a magnified (larger) image of your teeth and see clearly.
  • Telescopes: Large curved concave mirrors collect light from distant stars and help astronomers observe them.
  • Solar Cookers & Solar Furnaces: Concave mirrors focus sunlight on one spot to produce heat for cooking or melting metals.
  • Shaving/Make-up Mirrors: A concave mirror held close gives a large, erect (upright) image — great for detailed work.
Uses of Convex Mirror
  • Side-View Mirrors in Vehicles: Convex mirrors give a wider field of view. Drivers can see more of the road behind them. This is why they say "Objects in mirror are closer than they appear."
  • Security Mirrors in Shops: Convex mirrors placed at shop corners allow shopkeepers to see a wide area of the shop.
  • ATM Mirrors: Small convex mirrors placed near ATMs help users see who is standing behind them.
Why Concave Mirrors are NOT Used as Rear-View Mirrors

Concave mirrors form inverted (upside-down) images when objects are far away. This would confuse drivers. Convex mirrors always show erect, smaller images and a wider area — making them safer and more practical for driving.

SECTION 07 Refraction of Light
Definition

Refraction is the bending of light when it passes from one medium to another (e.g., from air to glass, or from air to water). The bending happens because light changes its speed as it moves from one medium to another.

Cause of Refraction

Light travels at different speeds in different mediums:

  • In air/vacuum → speed is highest (3 × 10⁸ m/s)
  • In water → speed decreases
  • In glass → speed decreases even more

When light enters a denser medium (like glass), it slows down and bends towards the normal. When it enters a rarer medium (like air from glass), it speeds up and bends away from the normal.

Daily Life Examples of Refraction
  • A pencil placed in a glass of water looks broken or bent at the surface — this is refraction.
  • A pool of water looks shallower than it actually is because of refraction.
  • Mirages in deserts or on hot roads — light bends through layers of air at different temperatures.
  • Stars appear to twinkle because their light refracts through different layers of the atmosphere.
  • A magnifying glass works by refracting light through a convex lens to make objects appear larger.

SECTION 08 Lenses
Definition of Lens

A lens is a piece of transparent material (like glass or plastic) with one or two curved surfaces. Unlike a mirror which reflects light, a lens refracts (bends) light that passes through it.

A Simple Experiment — Water Drop Lens!

Place a round drop of water on a flat strip of glass over some printed text. Look through the water drop. The text appears bigger! That is because the curved surface of the water drop acts like a convex lens — it bends light and magnifies the image. This is exactly how a magnifying glass works.

Types of Lenses
Convex Lens

A lens that is thicker in the middle and thinner at the edges. It is also called a converging lens because it bends light inward and brings rays together at a point (focus).

Examples: Magnifying glass, camera lens, human eye lens, spectacles for far-sightedness.

Concave Lens

A lens that is thinner in the middle and thicker at the edges. It is also called a diverging lens because it bends light outward and spreads rays apart.

Examples: Spectacles for short-sightedness (myopia), spy-holes in doors.

Lens and the Human Eye

The human eye contains a natural convex lens. This lens bends the light entering the eye and focuses it on the retina (back of the eye) to form a clear image. Problems with the eye's lens cause vision problems:

  • Short-sightedness (Myopia) — Cannot see far objects clearly. Corrected using a concave lens (diverging lens) in spectacles.
  • Far-sightedness (Hypermetropia) — Cannot see near objects clearly. Corrected using a convex lens (converging lens) in spectacles.

Short Answer Questions (2–3 Marks)
Q1. What are the characteristics of the image formed by a plane mirror?
Ans: (1) Virtual and erect. (2) Same size as the object. (3) Formed at the same distance behind the mirror as the object is in front. (4) Laterally inverted.
Q2. Why do convex mirrors always form smaller images?
Ans: Convex mirrors have a curved-outward surface that diverges (spreads) reflected light rays. Because the rays spread apart, they appear to come from a point behind the mirror, forming a smaller, virtual, erect image — regardless of how far or close the object is.
Q3. Differentiate between a real image and a virtual image.
Ans: Real image — formed by actual meeting of reflected rays; can be caught on a screen; always inverted. Virtual image — formed where reflected rays appear to meet (but don't actually); cannot be caught on a screen; always erect.
Q4. What is refraction? Give one example.
Ans: Refraction is the bending of light when it travels from one medium to another. It happens because light changes speed. Example: A pencil placed in a glass of water appears bent at the surface — this is due to refraction.

Q1. Explain the laws of reflection with a labelled diagram.
Ans: When light hits a mirror surface, it reflects according to two laws: Law 1: The angle of incidence (∠i) = angle of reflection (∠r). The angle of incidence is the angle between the incident ray and the normal; the angle of reflection is the angle between the reflected ray and the normal. Law 2: The incident ray, the reflected ray, and the normal at the point of incidence — all lie in the same plane.
